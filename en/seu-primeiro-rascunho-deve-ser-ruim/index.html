Your First Draft Should Be Bad

There was a time when I thought being a perfectionist was synonymous with quality. That it meant being someone who wouldn’t settle for the bare minimum or mediocrity, someone who was always striving for the best possible result.

It made me feel disciplined, committed, like someone who wouldn’t rest until she had delivered her best work.

The best…

A while ago, I started working with a colleague who, whenever I said something could be improved, would ask me a question: better for whom?

It sounds like a simple question, but it made me rethink a lot of things.

When we work with digital products, we have to balance an enormous number of expectations. We have deadlines to meet, business goals to achieve, clients to satisfy, users who need to be able to use what we create, and developers who need to understand how to turn our ideas into something functional.

And in the middle of all that, we still want to create something beautiful, interesting, and something we can proudly say: I made that.

See how many people we’re trying to please with our work?

Working in design means constantly exposing our ideas to other people’s judgment. And people will always have an opinion. There will always be someone who would have chosen a different color, prioritized another feature, used a different component, or taken an entirely different approach.

And sometimes, that other approach might actually be better.

That’s a lot of pressure, isn’t it?

I think that’s a big part of why so many creative professionals are perfectionists about their work. We’re afraid of disappointing people. Afraid of criticism, of looking incompetent, of finding out that the idea we thought was so great might not be all that great after all.

But there’s a very simple solution to this problem: if our work is perfect, no one will be able to criticize it.

Problem solved. Just make it perfect!

There’s just one tiny problem with that: perfection doesn’t exist.

And I don’t say that simply because there will always be something we can improve, but because the very idea of perfection changes depending on who’s looking at it.

What’s perfect for the business might not be perfect for the user. What’s perfect for the designer might be a nightmare for the developer. And what seems like the best possible solution today might no longer make sense a few months from now.

So, if perfection depends on so many variables, what exactly are we trying to achieve?

Fear Disguised as Quality

I’ve been thinking that, quite often, what we call perfectionism is just a nicer way of talking about fear.

Maybe I spent too much time trying to achieve some kind of universal approval. I was terrified that someone more experienced would look at my work, find something wrong with it, and think: “She has no idea what she’s doing.”

I started my career as a designer without a formal education in design, and I felt like I had to make up for that somehow. So doing work that was so good no one could question it seemed like a great approach.

But imagine how ridiculous it is to think that you, of all people, somehow need to know everything, and that no one around you is allowed to know more than you do. It sounds almost pathetic when you say it out loud, but that was the expectation I had placed on myself.

The truth is, there will always be someone who knows more, who has different experiences, a different perspective, or who would simply do things differently. And realizing that, rather than discouraging me, was incredibly liberating.

Because no matter how much I learn, there will always be more to learn. And thank goodness for that! These days, I find it incredibly limiting to believe that we could reach a point where we know enough, or where our work has finally reached its best possible version.

After all, if we’ve already reached the best, what comes next?

The funny thing is that we, as designers, should be quite familiar with this idea. We work through iterative processes and develop products that are constantly being updated and improved. We know that a solution that works perfectly well today might need to be reconsidered tomorrow, because needs change, technology evolves, and we learn new things along the way.

But if we understand so well that a product doesn’t need to start out in its final form, why do we struggle so much to accept the same thing when it comes to our own projects?

Maybe it’s because there’s a huge difference between wanting to do good work and feeling like our work is never good enough to be shown.

In the first case, we’re trying to improve the outcome. In the second, we’re finding reasons to delay putting it out into the world.

And the funny thing is that the second one can actually look productive. After all, we’re working, reviewing, looking for inspiration, exploring new possibilities, tweaking details…

How could we possibly be procrastinating when we’re so busy?

But we are.

It’s procrastination disguised as dedication. It’s not that we don’t get things done because we don’t care. Quite the opposite. We care so much about the outcome that we end up never getting there.

And while we’re revising something that already works for the tenth time, we’re missing out on exploring other ideas, starting new projects, and learning things we would only discover by putting our work out into the world.

Perhaps that’s one of the most frustrating things about perfectionism: it convinces us that we’re making progress when, quite often, we’re just going around in circles.

The Never-Ending Story of the Perfect Portfolio

If you’re a designer, you probably already know where I’m going with this.

The portfolio.

Ah, the portfolio…

That one project every designer needs to have, but that never seems to be finished.

First, you decide it’s time to update your projects. But since you’re already updating them, maybe it’s a good opportunity to rethink your visual identity. And if you’re changing your visual identity, why not redesign the entire layout while you’re at it? Maybe that old project no longer reflects your skills. Better put together a new case study. And that amazing reference you just found on Pinterest? That’s definitely going in.

Before you know it, you’re building an entirely new portfolio.

Again.

I started mine from scratch five times before I finally managed to finish and publish a version.

Five.

And I’m not talking about five minor layout changes. I’m talking about starting all over again because, at some point in the process, what I was building no longer seemed good enough.

The process was almost always the same: I’d start out excited, look through a thousand references, put together moodboards, experiment with layouts, and imagine all the possibilities for the website. But when it came time to select and write about my projects, nothing seemed to live up to what I had imagined.

One project needed a better presentation, another could use a few adjustments, and that older one perhaps didn’t represent my work all that well anymore…

And suddenly, what was supposed to be the creation of a portfolio turned into a complete overhaul of several projects at once. The process kept getting bigger and more exhausting, and that initial excitement eventually gave way to frustration.

The worst part was that I was still working quite a lot. I was reviewing, adjusting, refining, but not necessarily making progress on what I had originally set out to do.

It was as if my portfolio needed to represent not only the designer I was at that moment, but also every version of the designer I might become.

How on earth is one website supposed to do all that?

The truth is that while I was trying to build the best possible representation of my work, I didn’t have a finished version to show anyone.

The very thing that was supposed to help me show what I was capable of had become one of the biggest obstacles to doing so.

The Courage to Call Something Finished

After so many attempts, I decided to do things differently.

The content had always been the hardest part, so I decided to start there. But this time, without worrying about getting every word right. I just wrote, and wrote, and wrote, and wrote. Without stopping to review every sentence, correct every little detail, or decide whether it was good enough.

I just wanted to have something to work with later. In fact, that’s exactly how I’m writing this post.

When I finished, I had plenty of content. And for the first time, instead of starting all over again, I had something to review, organize, and improve.

Little by little, I managed to put together a complete first version of my portfolio. It wasn’t perfect, but it was finished, I had made it myself, and I was so proud to have finally done it.

And I don’t think that pride had as much to do with the result as it did with actually finishing something. I’ve always been a person with many interests, but also many unfinished projects. Books, drawings, musical instruments… the list goes on.

So finally completing something I had been putting off for so long felt like quite an accomplishment.

But there was still one equally terrifying part left: showing my work to other people.

Remember that fear of criticism, of someone looking at our work and thinking we don’t know what we’re doing? Well, after spending so much time trying to avoid that judgment, I decided to do exactly the opposite: reach out to people with more experience and basically say, “Go ahead, judge me.”

I knew asking for feedback could help me. I just couldn’t bring myself to take that first step.

So, since I didn’t feel quite ready to show my portfolio to the world, I started by showing it to a small part of it.

And guess what? No one said I didn’t know what I was doing. No one questioned my abilities. People simply shared their thoughts, suggested improvements, and helped me move forward.

Was that really what I’d been so afraid of?

The very thing I had spent so much time trying to avoid turned out to be exactly what helped me move forward.

When Improving Is No Longer the Goal

I don’t think the solution is to simply stop caring about the quality of things.

After all, there’s a lot of value in dedicating time to an idea, exploring possibilities, and paying attention to the details. Quite often, that attention to detail is precisely what separates mediocre work from genuinely good work.

But we also need to learn to recognize when a change is actually improving the outcome and when we’re simply trying to ease our own insecurities.

And this is where that question becomes relevant again.

Better for whom?

If an interface already meets the users’ needs, solves the business problem, and can be implemented properly, is that adjustment we keep insisting on really going to make it better?

Or are we trying to live up to an expectation that only exists in our heads?

I think we need to learn to live with the idea that something can be finished without being perfect. My portfolio doesn’t need to be the definitive version of my work.

If all goes well, the designer I am a year from now will look at this website and find plenty of things she’d do differently. (And thank goodness for that!)

I’m still learning how to deal with this. I’m still someone who notices the little details, enjoys exploring possibilities, and occasionally spends more time than she should trying to solve something that was probably already solved.

But I’m more aware of the cost of that now. And that cost isn’t just measured in the hours we spend on a project, but also in the ideas we never get to explore, the opportunities we miss, and the energy we spend trying to avoid criticism that might never come.

And if it does, that’s okay. It might even help us make something better.

Today, my portfolio is live. It’s not perfect, it’s not final, and there’s probably already something I want to change as I’m writing this.

But it exists. People can visit it, explore my work, and draw their own conclusions.

And that’s far more than any of the five versions I left behind ever managed to do.

And since I’ve spent this entire post talking about overcoming the fear of showing our work, I think it’s only fair to end by doing exactly that: you’re invited to check out my portfolio!

Just keep in mind that it’s still a work in progress (and probably always will be). I’m still making improvements, trying new things, and learning along the way. So if you have any thoughts, suggestions, or constructive criticism, I’d love to hear them.

Here’s to letting ourselves make more bad first drafts. That’s where the good ones come from.
